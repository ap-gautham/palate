\begin{abstract}
Two fully parallel projects predict a person's rating of a film from other raters: critic pseudo-users on Rotten Tomatoes (301 test critics, 43{,}344 paired episodes) and real members on Letterboxd (300 test members, 41{,}058 paired episodes). Raters are partitioned 70/15/15 into train/validation/test first, and pseudo-profiles are then amplified within each partition into one cached, seeded training pool per project (\S\ref{sec:protocol}), so no test rater's ratings ever reach training. Three designs compete on identical held-out episodes in each project: an analytic movie-mean-centered formula (with a second variant restricted to the 10 most-aligned-or-anti-aligned peers), an XGBoost regressor, and a residual neural-network ensemble -- all sharing one engineered feature contract that includes, beyond similarity and consensus, real content-based affinity features joined from the gsimonx37/letterboxd Kaggle metadata dump: a user-relative z-scored affinity per genre, a sentence-embedding theme-similarity estimate, per-actor affinity (by rating and by viewing count) with cast-overlap statistics, and per-director affinity (\S\ref{sec:features-eq}). On Rotten Tomatoes, full-history RMSE is 0.801 (Design 1), 0.783 (XGBoost), 0.789 (neural net), all beating the 0.843--0.853 flat baselines -- the best model is \textbf{7.1\% more accurate} than simply quoting each film's mean reviewer score. On Letterboxd, full-history RMSE is 1.502, 1.417, 1.418 against a 1.654 flat baseline -- a \textbf{14.3\%} improvement for the best model. Every design also trains a parallel z-score track (each rater standardized to their own scale, predicting pure deviation, converted back before scoring) to test whether the gain is calibration or taste-matching; the result is not uniform across datasets or designs, reported as found (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\end{abstract}

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Rotten Tomatoes}
\label{sec:data-rt}

Fractions, letter grades, percentages, and star ratings are parsed and quantized to $\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$, the \emph{standardized raw score} -- the prediction target. Critics with at most five parseable scores are excluded, name variants are deduplicated, and repeat critic--movie cells are averaged.

The data retain a diagnostic z-score:
\begin{equation}
z_{c,i}=\frac{r_{c,i}-\mu_c}{\sigma_c}.
\label{eq:zscore}
\end{equation}
Here $r_{c,i}$ is critic $c$'s standardized raw score for movie $i$; $\mu_c$ is critic $c$'s all-time mean score; and $\sigma_c$ is critic $c$'s all-time standard deviation. The \emph{Tomatometer} is the percentage of reviews labelled Fresh, not a mean 0--5 rating; baseline B2 fits $t_m=aT_m+b$ (a linear calibration of movie $m$'s Tomatometer percentage onto the raw score scale).

\textbf{Movie facets.} RT's own \texttt{genre} column is a comma-separated multi-genre string, parsed in full (not just the first entry). This is joined to the gsimonx37/letterboxd Kaggle metadata dump (\texttt{genres}, \texttt{themes}, \texttt{actors}, \texttt{crew}, \texttt{languages} -- \texttt{studios}/\texttt{countries} are downloaded but not used, and posters are excluded entirely) by normalized (title, year), with title-only fallback when a year is missing (RT's own release-date field is populated for only 30{,}512 of 142{,}052 movies). Five facets survive into the feature contract: genre, theme, language, director, and actor (the 20 most-billed actors per film -- see \S\ref{sec:features-eq}); decade and studio/country are excluded, decade because \texttt{year} already carries that signal and studio/country because they cost more columns than they were worth. The join matches \textbf{69.2\%} of RT's full movie catalog and \textbf{99.3\%} of the 1{,}000-film app catalog (popular films skew toward better metadata coverage); unmatched films fall back to RT's own genre string and empty facets elsewhere, reported honestly rather than assumed complete. Rating-weighted across all scored reviews (i.e. weighting each film's coverage by how often it was actually rated, not by raw film count), \textbf{67.2\%} of reviewed films have at least one theme and \textbf{82.3\%} have at least one credited actor; among films with only 3 or more raters, theme coverage drops to \textbf{36.2\%} -- the theme affinity feature (\S\ref{sec:features-eq}) is \texttt{NaN} whenever neither the target nor any seen film has themes.

\subsection{Letterboxd}
\label{sec:data-lb}

To test whether real, dense per-user rating histories beat the sparse critic pseudo-users above, a fully parallel second project was built on Letterboxd community ratings (Kaggle, \textasciitilde11.08M ratings), isolated in its own directory tree (\texttt{src/letterboxd/}, \texttt{data/letterboxd/}, \texttt{results/letterboxd/}) sharing no data, models, or code with Rotten Tomatoes. The export yields 7{,}420 members with at least five rated films across 286{,}069 films -- 11{,}078{,}045 ratings total. Ratings are native on a 1--10 scale (no score parsing, no Tomatometer feature).

The same gsimonx37 join is repeated independently (title, year; LB's own \texttt{movie\_id} slug already encodes the year, so the join is more reliable here) and matches \textbf{94.3\%} of LB's full catalog and \textbf{99.8\%} of the 1{,}000-film app catalog. LB's own \texttt{genres} column (a JSON list, already multi-genre) is the fallback when gsimonx37 misses. Coverage is denser here than on RT (LB's join is more reliable, and its raters skew toward well-catalogued films): rating-weighted, \textbf{78.8\%} of rated films have at least one theme and \textbf{96.4\%} have at least one credited actor; among films with only 3 or more raters, theme coverage is \textbf{15.4\%}.

\section{Equations and concepts}
\label{sec:concepts}

\subsection{Similarity and magnitude}

Let $O_{u,c}$ be movies rated by pseudo-user/visitor $u$ and peer (critic/member) $c$ among the sampled seen movies. Pearson correlation is
\begin{equation}
\rho_{u,c}=
\frac{\sum_{i\in O_{u,c}}(r_{u,i}-\bar r_{u,O})(r_{c,i}-\bar r_{c,O})}
{\sqrt{\sum_{i\in O_{u,c}}(r_{u,i}-\bar r_{u,O})^2}
 \sqrt{\sum_{i\in O_{u,c}}(r_{c,i}-\bar r_{c,O})^2}}.
\label{eq:pearson}
\end{equation}
Small overlaps are shrunk toward zero:
\begin{equation}
s_{u,c}=\rho_{u,c}\frac{\min(n_{u,c},k)}{k},\qquad n_{u,c}=|O_{u,c}|,\ k=8.
\label{eq:shrink}
\end{equation}
The rating-magnitude multiplier (a least-squares through-origin mapping from peer to user ratings) is
\begin{equation}
g_{u,c}=\frac{\sum_{i\in O_{u,c}}r_{u,i}r_{c,i}}{\sum_{i\in O_{u,c}}r_{c,i}^{2}}.
\label{eq:magnitude}
\end{equation}
Pearson correlation is affine-invariant, so $\rho_{u,c}$ (and $s_{u,c}$) is \emph{identical} whether computed in raw units or z-units; only $g_{u,c}$ and the target consensus differ between the raw and z-score tracks (\S\ref{sec:zscore-concept}).

\subsection{Movie-mean-centered prediction and the top-$|$sim$|$ variant}
\label{sec:analytic-eq}

For target movie $m$, let $C_m$ be other peers who rated it, with movie mean $\bar r_m=\frac{1}{|C_m|}\sum_{c\in C_m}r_{c,m}$. The full-neighbourhood prediction is
\begin{equation}
\hat r_{u,m}=\frac{\sum_{c\in C_m}\left(|s_{u,c}|\bar r_m+s_{u,c}(r_{c,m}-\bar r_m)\right)g_{u,c}}{\sum_{c\in C_m}|s_{u,c}|},
\label{eq:prediction}
\end{equation}
clipped to the dataset's rating range; a zero denominator falls back to $\bar r_m$.

A second, new variant restricts $C_m$ to $C_m^{(k)}\subseteq C_m$, the $k=10$ peers with the largest $|s_{u,c}|$ -- both strongly aligned ($s_{u,c}\gg0$) \emph{and} strongly anti-aligned ($s_{u,c}\ll0$) peers, not just the most positively-aligned like baseline B4:
\begin{equation}
C_m^{(k)}=\operatorname*{arg\,top\text{-}k}_{c\in C_m}\ |s_{u,c}|,
\qquad
\hat r_{u,m}^{(k)}=\frac{\sum_{c\in C_m^{(k)}}\left(|s_{u,c}|\bar r_m^{(k)}+s_{u,c}(r_{c,m}-\bar r_m^{(k)})\right)g_{u,c}}{\sum_{c\in C_m^{(k)}}|s_{u,c}|},
\label{eq:topk}
\end{equation}
where $\bar r_m^{(k)}$ is the mean over the restricted set $C_m^{(k)}$. Equation~\ref{eq:topk} is otherwise identical to Equation~\ref{eq:prediction} -- same shrinkage, same magnitude term -- just over a smaller, alignment-selected neighbourhood. The original full-neighbourhood formula is unchanged and remains the default; the top-$|$sim$|$ variant is purely additive, computed identically for both datasets.

\subsection{Feature engineering}
\label{sec:features-eq}

Every trained design (Design 2 and 3) sees the same fixed-width row, built once here and consumed identically by both. It has four parts: a peer-similarity summary, target-side scalars, user-relative content affinities (the focus of this section), and target-side metadata.

\textbf{Decile summary (30 columns).} Each peer's target-movie deviation is its leave-one-out all-time mean subtracted from its target score. Peers are ranked by $s_{u,c}$ and their $s_{u,c}\times\text{deviation}$ products are summarized (mean, count, standard deviation) in ten similarity-ranked deciles -- identical construction for both datasets and both raw/z tracks.

\textbf{Target-side scalars (7/6 columns).} Seen count, mean/max peer overlap, peer count, the target's rating dispersion across all its raters, mapped Tomatometer (RT only), and the user's own mean rating. No single-genre ordinal is included: the 40-column genre affinity block below covers every genre a film has (not just its first), so a separate \texttt{genre\_id} column would be redundant.

\textbf{Content affinities (77 columns).} Built from the gsimonx37 movie-facet join (\S\ref{sec:data-rt}, \S\ref{sec:data-lb}). A design note on why these are per-value blocks rather than a coarser ``mean deviation over any shared facet value'' aggregate: the aggregate form cannot distinguish a film matching the target on two facet values from one matching on one, and its zero-count case is ambiguous between ``no shared value'' and ``target has no data for this facet.'' Each block instead computes, per user-relative quantity, a genuine estimate of how the user has rated similar content before, always paired with a confidence count that doubles as its own missingness indicator (a NaN average's paired count is always exactly 0 or, for genre, at most 1 -- see below). Let $\mu_u,\sigma_u$ be the mean and standard deviation of the user's own seen-set ratings for this episode (raw track: the actual seen ratings; z-track: the already-standardized per-episode z-values, so $\mu_u\approx0,\sigma_u\approx1$ there by construction); when $\sigma_u<10^{-9}$ (a seen set with no rating variance, most often $n=1$), $\sigma_u$ falls back to $\sigma_{\text{global}}$, the standard deviation of every rating in the dataset.

\emph{Genre (40 columns).} Let $G$ be the canonical genre vocabulary -- the 19 named gsimonx37 genres plus one \texttt{"\_\_other\_\_"} slot, so $|G|=20$ regardless of catalog. For genre $g\in G$ and seen set $S$ with per-film ratings $r_i$:
\begin{equation}
z_g(u,m)=\frac{\bar r_{H_g}-\mu_u}{\sigma_u},\qquad H_g=\{i\in S: g\in F_{\text{genre}}(i)\},
\label{eq:genrez}
\end{equation}
undefined (\texttt{NaN}) when $H_g=\varnothing$; $\bar r_{H_g}$ is the mean seen rating over $H_g$. The paired count \emph{includes the target film itself}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{cnt}_g(u,m)=\log\!\bigl(1+|H_g|+\mathbb{1}[g\in F_{\text{genre}}(m)]\bigr).
\label{eq:genrecnt}
\end{equation}
This single count column replaces a separate target-side multi-hot entirely: $\mathrm{cnt}_g=0$ means the target isn't genre $g$; $\mathrm{cnt}_g=\log 1$ (i.e. the $+1$ alone) means the target is genre $g$ but the user has seen none; $\mathrm{cnt}_g=\log(1+n)$ for $n\geq1$ seen films means both. It is target-side information only ($\mathbb{1}[g\in F_{\text{genre}}(m)]$ needs no rating), so it adds no leakage.

\emph{Theme (3 columns).} The 109 gsimonx37 theme phrases are short descriptive English sentences (e.g. \emph{``Gothic and eerie haunting horror,''} \emph{``Terrifying, haunted, and supernatural horror''}) -- similar in meaning but sharing no facet string, so a set-overlap affinity (as genre/director/actor use) would miss the relationship entirely. Each phrase is instead encoded once, offline, with a sentence-transformer (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}, L2-normalized), giving a $109\times109$ cosine similarity matrix computed a single time and cached (seconds on CPU); the two horror phrases above cosine at $0.79$, against $0.21$--$0.27$ to an unrelated theme -- concrete evidence the embedding captures what string matching cannot. For target themes $T_m$ and a seen film $i$ with themes $T_i$, define $w(t,i)=\max_{\tau\in T_i}\cos(e_t,e_\tau)$ for $t\in T_m$ (0 if $T_i=\varnothing$). The theme feature is a similarity-weighted average of the \emph{raw} seen rating (not a deviation -- it is a kernel-smoothed rating estimate over the theme graph, meant to be directly comparable to the prediction target):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{avg}_\theta(u,m)=\frac{\sum_{t\in T_m}\sum_{i\in S} w(t,i)\,r_i}{\sum_{t\in T_m}\sum_{i\in S} w(t,i)},
\label{eq:themeavg}
\end{equation}
undefined when $T_m=\varnothing$ or the denominator is 0. (This is algebraically the mass-weighted combination of each theme's own weighted average -- the per-theme intermediate cancels.) The mass itself, $\log(1+\sum_{t,i}w(t,i))$, is the confidence companion and the exact mask for Equation~\ref{eq:themeavg} (zero iff the average is NaN); a third column counts (log1p) seen films with $\max_{t\in T_m} w(t,i)>0.8$, i.e. strongly on-theme.

\emph{Actor (30 columns).} Cast depth is the 20 most-billed actors per film -- deep enough that cast-overlap statistics carry signal (the gsimonx37 dump's median cast is 6, 90th percentile 20; a 2--3-actor cast would make overlap nearly always empty). Three top-5 rankings of the target's cast against the user's seen history: (i) by the user's mean rating for that actor, tie-broken by count; (ii) by the user's viewing count for that actor, tie-broken by mean rating; (iii) seen films ranked by shared-cast count with the target, keeping each film's own raw rating (a single observation, not averaged -- \texttt{user\_mean} already carries the user's general level). Each ranking contributes a z-score/rating and a log1p count per rank; unused ranks (fewer than 5 matching actors/films) are padded with \texttt{NaN} (z-scores and ratings) and 0 (counts).

\emph{Director (2 columns).} One z-score and one log1p(count), identical construction to genre/actor, over seen films sharing any director with the target (no target-inclusive count here -- directors are near-unique per film, so the genre trick's leakage-free target bit adds little).

\textbf{Numeric tail (4/4 columns).} $\log(1+\text{runtime})$, the gsimonx37 dataset's own average site rating for the film, and $\log(1+n_{\text{themes}})$, $\log(1+n_{\text{languages}})$.

\textbf{Missing-value convention.} XGBoost splits on \texttt{NaN} natively (missing-value direction), so it needs no imputation. The neural network cannot use \texttt{NaN} directly, so every \texttt{NaN}-able column is imputed with a fixed sentinel before standardization: rating-scale averages (theme average, cast-overlap ratings) get $-1$ (impossible on both the 0--5 and 1--10 scales); z-scores get $0$ (their own neutral point -- ``no evidence'' and ``exactly average'' are deliberately not distinguished further, since the paired count already grades how much evidence there is). No separate missingness mask is used: every \texttt{NaN}-able column already has a count companion that \emph{is} its mask, and the count does more than flag absence -- it grades how weak the evidence is, so zero, one, and two prior observations fall in one smoothly learnable region rather than requiring a hard binary split.

In total the feature row has \textbf{116} columns on Rotten Tomatoes (with Tomatometer) and \textbf{115} on Letterboxd.

\subsection{The z-score track}
\label{sec:zscore-concept}

Every design also trains a parallel z-score variant to test directly whether its gain over the baselines is level-calibration or genuine taste-matching. Each rater is standardized to their own scale using \textbf{two different conventions}, deliberately:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The user} (the fake pseudo-user offline, or the real visitor in the app) is standardized by the mean/std of \emph{only the ratings sampled as seen for this episode} -- never a critic/member's all-time stats -- since a real visitor only ever has their own seen films to standardize against: $z_{u,i}=(r_{u,i}-\mu_u)/\sigma_u$ where $\mu_u,\sigma_u$ come from that episode's seen set.
\item \textbf{Peers} (critics/members) are standardized by their \emph{all-time} mean/std -- Equation~\ref{eq:zscore} for RT, the analogous all-time computation for LB.
\end{itemize}
The model predicts pure deviation $\hat z$ in this mixed z-space, then converts back to the raw scale before scoring:
\begin{equation}
\hat r = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\mu_u + \sigma_u\hat z,\ \text{range}\right).
\label{eq:convertback}
\end{equation}
$\mathrm{user\_mean}\approx0$ by construction on this track (the level was removed), and the content-affinity blocks (\S\ref{sec:features-eq}) use $z_{u,i}$ directly as the seen-set "rating" they average over -- already $\approx0$-mean, $\approx1$-std, so those blocks are already scale-free on this track without any extra transformation. RMSE is always reported after Equation~\ref{eq:convertback}, so raw-track and z-track numbers are directly comparable (\S\ref{sec:zscore-results}). An episode is skipped on the z-track only if its seen ratings have zero variance ($\sigma_u=0$, can't standardize).

\subsection{Sampling protocol and the training pool}
\label{sec:protocol}

\textbf{Split first, then amplify.} Every rater (critic on Rotten Tomatoes, member on Letterboxd) is assigned to exactly one of train/validation/test by a deterministic seeded shuffle -- \textbf{70\,/\,15\,/\,15} in both projects. On Rotten Tomatoes the 2{,}894 pseudo-user critics split 2{,}026\,/\,434\,/\,434; on Letterboxd the 7{,}409 eligible members ($\geq$6 ratings) split 5{,}186\,/\,1{,}111\,/\,1{,}112. All pseudo-profile sampling happens \emph{within} a partition, so every profile derived from one rater stays in that rater's split: no rating belonging to a test rater is ever visible at training time.

\textbf{Pseudo-profile amplification.} A single rater with a long history supports many distinct training examples: an episode fixes one held-out target film and samples $n$ of the rater's remaining films as the ``seen'' set. Training and validation rows draw, per rater, several independent random profiles at every seen-count in the evaluation grid $n\in\{3,5,10,20,50,\text{all}\}$ (32 train / 8 validation profiles per $n$ on Rotten Tomatoes; 4 / 2 on Letterboxd, whose raters are $\sim$2.5$\times$ more numerous and far denser). Every rater draws from its own RNG stream, seeded as \texttt{default\_rng([seed, rater\_position])} -- independent of processing order and worker count, so the pool is bit-for-bit reproducible whether generated serially or across processes.

\textbf{One cached pool, shared by both trained designs.} The full pool -- training, validation, and paired test rows, in both the raw and z-score tracks -- is generated once by a dedicated pipeline stage (\texttt{build\_rows}, \texttt{make rt-rows}\,/\,\texttt{lb-rows}) and cached to parquet with a manifest recording the seed and a hash of the feature contract. XGBoost and the neural network both load this cache, so the two trained designs fit on byte-identical rows and are scored on byte-identical test episodes; a stale cache (changed contract or seed) is refused rather than silently regenerated. The pool sizes: 358{,}080 training rows per track on Rotten Tomatoes and $\sim$125{,}000 on Letterboxd (every train member contributes, capped only by each member's own history length). A raw-track row and its z-track twin are built from the \emph{same} sampled episode, and an episode is dropped from both tracks if either row is unavailable, keeping the tracks 1:1 comparable.

\textbf{Paired, nested test episodes.} Test raters are evaluated on the shared paired protocol (\S\ref{sec:design1}--\ref{sec:design3}): per eligible test rater (more than 50 rated films), 8 fixed targets $\times$ 3 popularity-weighted seen-order redraws, with the seen set at count $n$ a nested prefix of the same order -- so every seen-count, every design, and every baseline is scored on an identical (rater, target, draw) set.

\subsection{Metrics}

Primary error is
\begin{equation}
\rmsen=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\hat r_j-y_j)^2}{N}},
\label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
where $j$ indexes an episode. Two secondary metrics (\S\ref{sec:app-limits}) check whether the gain is calibration or reordering: within-user Spearman rank correlation, and precision@10 (overlap between a user's predicted and true top-10 held-out films).

\section{Design 1: analytic formula}
\label{sec:design1}

\subsection{Raw}

Design 1 is Equation~\ref{eq:prediction} (full neighbourhood) and its new top-$|$sim$|$ variant, Equation~\ref{eq:topk}, computed identically on both datasets from the same similarity/magnitude substrate (\S\ref{sec:concepts}). No trained parameters beyond the validation-selected shrinkage constant $k=8$ (Equation~\ref{eq:shrink}) and the neighbourhood size $k=10$ for the top-$|$sim$|$ variant. B4 (the existing baseline) selects the $k$ most \emph{positively}-aligned peers and averages unweighted; the new Design-1 variant selects by $|s_{u,c}|$ (so it can include strongly anti-aligned peers) and applies the full weighted formula, not a plain average.

\subsection{Z-score}

Both the full-neighbourhood and top-$|$sim$|$ formulas are recomputed against the peer pool's all-time z-values (Equation~\ref{eq:zscore}) instead of raw scores, with $g_{u,c}$ and the target consensus in z-units; the shrunk similarity $s_{u,c}$ is unchanged (affine invariance, \S\ref{sec:concepts}). The z-space prediction is converted back via Equation~\ref{eq:convertback} using \emph{this episode's} seen-set $\mu_u,\sigma_u$ before clipping and scoring.

\section{Design 2: XGBoost}
\label{sec:design2}

\subsection{Raw}

An XGBoost regressor (scikit-learn estimator API, \texttt{XGBRegressor}) over the full 116/115-column feature row (\S\ref{sec:features-eq}): the 30 decile columns, seen-count/overlap/dispersion/reviewer-count, mapped Tomatometer (RT only), the 40-column genre block, the 3-column theme block, the 30-column actor block, the 2-column director block, and the 4-column numeric tail. Missing Tomatometer and every \texttt{NaN}-able affinity column are handled natively by XGBoost's missing-value split direction. Trained with early stopping on validation RMSE. Both tracks are trained as regressors rather than multiclass classifiers, even though the raw targets fall on a discrete grid (0--5 on Rotten Tomatoes, 1--10 on Letterboxd): the z-score track's target is continuous by construction, and the reported metric is RMSE on the original scale, which rewards ordered, interpolating predictions -- a multiclass objective would treat every misclassification as equally wrong regardless of how far off it is.

\subsection{Z-score}

A second XGBoost model is trained on the identical feature contract, but every peer-side quantity (deciles, facet peer-mean, tomatometer stays raw as an external feature) is built from the z-Split, and the target is $\hat z$; predictions are converted back via Equation~\ref{eq:convertback} before scoring, using the same episode's $\mu_u,\sigma_u$ as the raw track (so raw and z rows are drawn from byte-identical sampled episodes).

\section{Design 3: neural network}
\label{sec:design3}

\subsection{Raw}

A residual MLP ensemble in PyTorch (Apple GPU/MPS), architecturally identical for both datasets: the full 116/115 numeric columns -- no separate genre embedding, since the 40-column genre affinity block already gives the network richer per-genre information (every genre a film has, not just a single first-genre id) than an embedding lookup would -- log1p-compressed where they are counts, NaN-imputed with the fixed per-column sentinel of \S\ref{sec:features-eq} (not the column mean, so a missing affinity always lands at the same point regardless of what a given split's training rows happened to average), and standardized on train statistics, feed a projection directly into six pre-activation residual blocks of width 512 (3{,}238{,}353 weights per ensemble member; batch-norm, GELU, dropout), then a linear head. Trained with AdamW, ReduceLROnPlateau, and early stopping; three independently seeded networks are averaged into an ensemble. Both trained models are verified leakage-free: peer means are leave-one-out, $u$/the visitor is excluded everywhere, and every content-affinity block (\S\ref{sec:features-eq}) is built only from seen films.

\subsection{Z-score}

A second ensemble, identical architecture, trained on the z-Split's rows with target $\hat z$; the three z-track members are seeded from a distinct offset so they are not literal duplicates of the raw-track members. Predictions convert back via Equation~\ref{eq:convertback} before scoring, on the same paired test episodes as every other method.

\section{Similar-film suggestions: does rating them help?}
\label{sec:similar}

Each row of the app's ``films to predict'' table offers a ``movies like this one'' dropdown: rate one of the target film's nearest content neighbours and the prediction should, in principle, improve. Neighbours are precomputed offline with K-means: a standardized feature vector per catalog film (the genre multi-hot from \S\ref{sec:features-eq}, a catalog-scoped top-$k$ multi-hot over director/actor/theme/language, and numeric runtime/external-rating/year/consensus), clustered into $\max(2,\lfloor n/25\rfloor)$ groups, with each film's \texttt{k\_neighbors} nearest neighbours (Euclidean distance) taken from within its own cluster (topped up from the globally nearest remaining films if the cluster is too small). \texttt{k\_neighbors} is the dropdown's list length, identical on both datasets. Studio/country/decade are excluded from this vector for the same reasons they are excluded from the model feature contract (\S\ref{sec:features-eq}).

\textbf{Does it work?} 6{,}000 paired (rater, $n{=}10$ seen, target) episodes were drawn from each project's own 1{,}000-film catalog (Design 1, leave-one-out, identical protocol to \S\ref{sec:design1}) and bucketed by how many of the target's \texttt{k\_neighbors} nearest neighbours were already in that episode's seen set -- the same 6{,}000 predictions are reused for every \texttt{k\_neighbors} value, so only the bucketing (which films count as ``similar'') differs.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{plotD_similar_k_sweep.png}
\caption{Rotten Tomatoes: Design 1 RMSE by how many of a target's \texttt{k\_neighbors} nearest neighbours are already rated, for \texttt{k\_neighbors} $\in\{10,20,30\}$.}
\label{fig:plotD}
\end{figure}

On \textbf{Rotten Tomatoes} there is \emph{no clean beneficial signal}. At $k{=}20$ the per-bucket RMSE by similar-seen count is 0.796 (0 seen, $n{=}4{,}570$), 0.772 (1, $n{=}1{,}196$), 0.869 (2, $n{=}193$), 0.924 (3, $n{=}35$), 0.830 (4, $n{=}6$) -- essentially flat from 0 to 1 seen, then \emph{rising} as more similar films are rated, the opposite of the hoped-for direction, though the higher-count buckets are thin enough (35 and 6 episodes) that little should be read into the exact shape. Pooling every bucket with at least three similar films seen (dropping buckets under five episodes) gives one summary per $k$: 0.910 at $k{=}20$ ($n{=}41$) and 0.900 at $k{=}30$ ($n{=}89$), both \emph{above} the 0-seen baseline for their own $k$ (0.796 and 0.799) -- rating more of a film's nearest neighbours did not measurably improve its prediction in this sample.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{../results/letterboxd/figures/plotD_similar_k_sweep.png}
\caption{Letterboxd: the same sweep, styled identically to Figure~\ref{fig:plotD}.}
\label{fig:lb-plotD}
\end{figure}

On \textbf{Letterboxd}, the sweep is noisier still and shows no signal either: at $k{=}20$, RMSE by similar-seen count is 1.589 (0, $n{=}4{,}651$), 1.555 (1, $n{=}1{,}183$), 1.565 (2, $n{=}143$), 1.682 (3, $n{=}16$), 2.006 (4, $n{=}4$) -- flat within noise up to 2 seen, then rising on buckets thin enough (16 and 4 episodes) to be unreliable. The pooled $\geq$3-similar-seen summary is 1.682 at $k{=}20$ ($n{=}16$, below the 5-episode floor for a trustworthy weighted average so reported as a single bucket) and 1.588 at $k{=}30$ ($n{=}53$) -- both indistinguishable from, or slightly above, the 0-seen baseline (1.589 and 1.598). \textbf{Reported honestly}: on both datasets, rating a target film's nearest content neighbours first does not measurably sharpen the prediction in this sample -- a negative result for the ``movies like this one'' hypothesis, not a confirmation. \texttt{k\_neighbors}$=20$ is kept as the dropdown length on both projects regardless, since it is a UI choice (how many suggestions to show) independent of this null predictive-value finding. Full per-bucket numbers are in \texttt{results/\{rotten\_tomatoes,letterboxd\}/similar\_k\_sweep.csv} (\texttt{rotten\_tomatoes.similar\_k\_sweep} / \texttt{letterboxd.similar\_k\_sweep}).

\section{Application and limitations}
\label{sec:app-limits}

The website (Table~\ref{tab:funnel}: 1{,}000 catalog movies, 3{,}387 critics / 7{,}416 members) is organized in three sections, with a sort control (alphabetical, year, most-reviewed) and a \textbf{genre filter} (a dropdown built from each film's gsimonx37 genre list, \S\ref{sec:data-rt}--\ref{sec:data-lb}) over the search lists. \emph{(1) Films you have seen}: a search box adds a film to a table where each row carries a rating widget (five-star on Rotten Tomatoes, a 1--10 stepper on Letterboxd). \emph{(2) Films to predict}: the same searchable table, scoring optional; each row also has a ``movies like this one'' dropdown (\S\ref{sec:similar}) -- green if you have already rated one of its 20 nearest content neighbours, red otherwise. \emph{(3) Predictions}: for every target film the app shows all four model variants (Design 1 full, Design 1 top-$|$sim$|$, XGBoost, neural net) side by side, each with its raw and z-score prediction, plus the movie's consensus mean and (Rotten Tomatoes only) Tomatometer. When the visitor scores some target films, the app reports the mean squared error of every variant against the visitor's own score and marks the closest, answering ``which method predicts me best?''. All eight model variants (four designs $\times$ two tracks) run entirely client-side, ported from the Python training code to hand-written TypeScript (an XGBoost tree-walker, a neural-net forward pass, and the content-affinity feature computation of \S\ref{sec:features-eq}, verified to match Python to within numerical tie-break tolerance -- see \texttt{web/scripts/validate*.ts}). The theme block's sentence-transformer runs \emph{only offline}, once, over the 109 known theme phrases; the browser receives just the resulting $109\times109$ cosine-similarity matrix (a $\sim$48\,KB JSON file) and does a dot-product lookup, never transformer inference.

Two secondary metrics (\texttt{rotten\_tomatoes.analyze}, \texttt{secondary\_metrics.csv}) check that the gain is calibration rather than reordering: within-user Spearman rank correlation between predictions and held-out ratings, and precision@10. Design 1's within-user Spearman is 0.587 versus 0.578 for the reviewer mean (B3), and precision@10 is 0.287 versus 0.284 -- ranking is barely changed, consistent with the attribution finding (Table~\ref{tab:attr}) that the win is calibration, not reordering a user's films.

Item holdout is not temporal forecasting. The gsimonx37 join is fuzzy (normalized title + year) and RT's own release-date coverage is sparse, so its match rate (69.2\% full catalog) is lower than Letterboxd's (94.3\%) -- reported honestly rather than assumed complete; unmatched films fall back to each project's own (sparser) genre parsing. The top-$|$sim$|$ analytic variant underperforms the full formula on both datasets -- a negative result, not tuned away. The magnitude multiplier is an unregularized through-origin slope. Scores are quantized to six levels on Rotten Tomatoes; pseudo-users there are professional critics rather than casual users.

\subsection*{Repository layout}

Both projects use one flat module layout, file-for-file: \texttt{src/rotten\_tomatoes/} holds \texttt{analytic.py} (the formula, top-$k$ baseline, and the top-$|$sim$|$ variant), \texttt{train\_analytic.py} (the k-sweep and paired test), \texttt{build\_rows.py} (the cached training pool of \S\ref{sec:protocol}), \texttt{train\_xgboost.py}, \texttt{train\_neural.py} (with \texttt{network.py}), \texttt{analyze.py} (the cross-design comparison), \texttt{plots.py} (shared RMSE-by-$n$ plotting plus each trainer's per-run scratch plot), \texttt{attribution.py}, \texttt{similar\_k\_sweep.py} (the neighbour-list tuning of \S\ref{sec:similar}), the three \texttt{predict\_*.py} app-inference modules, and \texttt{preprocess.py}/\texttt{audit.py} for ingest. The pseudo-user substrate (\texttt{pseudo\_users.py}), the shared feature contract (\texttt{features.py}), and the gsimonx37 movie-facet join (\texttt{movie\_features.py}) live once at the package root and are imported by all three designs. \texttt{src/letterboxd/} mirrors this layout module for module (its own \texttt{pseudo\_users.py}, \texttt{features.py}, \texttt{movie\_features.py}, \texttt{build\_rows.py}, \texttt{train\_xgboost.py}, \texttt{train\_neural.py}, \texttt{analyze.py}, \texttt{web\_export.py}), sharing no code, data, or model artifacts with Rotten Tomatoes -- the gsimonx37 CSVs are the one shared external download, read independently by each project's own join. \texttt{tests/} covers the formula, the random-holdout and paired-episode protocols, partition disjointness, and the feature contract for both projects (\texttt{test\_design2\_features.py}, \texttt{test\_letterboxd\_features.py}) -- the genre count's leave-target-out property, the theme kernel's weighting and masking, the actor rankings' tie-breaks and padding, and the count-is-the-mask invariant across every affinity block.
